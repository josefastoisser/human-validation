Welcome to the **Prediction interval competition II: House price** competition, where the objective is to create a regression model for the sale price of a house that overall has the narrowest prediction intervals. 

The evaluation of this competition is a little different (see the metric below); in many machine learning contests involving regression it is not unusual to focus on what are called *point predictions* (expectation values) \\(\hat{Y}\\). However, in this competition we are uninterested in the expectation value, but rather in producing a prediction interval,  \\(\hat{C_{\alpha}}\\). Indeed prediction intervals are often of greater practical interest than point predictions, which only form part of the story.

Time-Aware Model Validation

Temporal Distribution and Trend

The dataset spans from 1999 to Jan 2025, with the test set extending 3 months beyond. While 98.6% of test data overlaps with training dates, about 1.42% is newer.

The sale prices show a clear upward trend over time, which implies the time component has a predictive signal. Ignoring it during validation can lead to overconfident models that perform well historically but degrade on newer data.

**Figure**: Median house Sale price over time

Why Careful Validation Matters

Validating models is always important—but in this competition, it's especially critical since we’re limited to one submission per day.

Why Standard Validation Fails

**Standard K-Fold Cross-Validation**: This approach doesn't test the model's ability to generalize to future data. While only 1.42% of the test set lies beyond the training date range, poor performance on this portion can still impact the final score.

**Time Series Split**: A chronological split will simulate testing the model on future data but it will overly penalize the model since 98.58% of test set is within the training date range.

A Balanced Validation Strategy

- **Determine a temporal cutoff**  

Identify a cutoff date such that the most recent **1.42%** of training data falls after this threshold—matching the proportion of future data in the test set.

- **Time-respecting K-Fold CV**  

Perform standard **K-Fold cross-validation** using training data **before the cutoff**. This estimates model's performance on data within training date range.

**Note:** Shuffle the data before CV to ensure folds represent a similar temporal distribution.

- **Holdout simulation of test leakage**  

Treat the training data **after the cutoff** as a **holdout validation set** to estimate model's performance on future data.

- **Final score aggregation**  

Compute the **Winkler score**  for both CV folds and holdout data. Then, calculate a **weighted average** of the fold and holdout scores, proportionally weighted by their sample sizes. This produces a composite score that balances model's performance within training date range and future data.

CV-LB Gap: An Analysis of Time-Based Validation

First, I'd like to give a huge thanks to [@omerabdelmoneim](https://www.kaggle.com/omerabdelmoneim) for his excellent [discussion post](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/prediction-interval-competition-ii-house-price/discussion/588017). It was a major source of inspiration for this analysis.

The Problem: A Consistent CV-LB Gap

It's a common observation in this competition that a gap exists between local cross-validation scores and the Public Leaderboard. For models validated with a standard `KFold`, a consistent gap often appears. For instance, a model achieving a local CV of **~302,000** scored **~306,800** on the Public LB. This analysis explores the idea that the way we handle **time** is key to understanding this gap.

The Experiment: KFold vs. TimeSeriesSplit

To investigate, a single model was tested with two different validation strategies:

- **Standard `KFold`:** With random shuffling, the model achieved a CV of **~302,000**. This method is prone to data leakage from the future, likely producing an overly optimistic score.

- **`TimeSeriesSplit`:** This method always uses past data to predict the future. As expected, the CV score worsened significantly to **~569,000**, which is likely a more realistic estimate of the model's true forecasting ability.

A Hypothesis: Calculating the Impact of Future Data

The full test set contains a small slice of "future" data (2025-02 to 2025-04) that constitutes **1.42%** of the total. Could this tiny but difficult portion account for the CV-LB gap?

Let's build a theory, starting with a key assumption: that the Public LB data also contains this 1.42% proportion of future data. We can then estimate a theoretical LB score:

- **For the "Interpolation" Part (98.58% of data):** Using the `KFold` CV as a proxy, the score is **~302,000**.

- **For the "Extrapolation" Part (1.42% of data):** Using the `TimeSeriesSplit` CV for forecasting performance, the score is **~569,000**.

The weighted average calculation is as follows:

`Expected LB = (0.9858 * 302,000) + (0.0142 * 569,000)`

`Expected LB = 297,711.6 + 8,079.8`

`Expected LB ≈ 305,791`

A Possible Explanation

The result of this calculation is a theoretical LB score of **~305,791**. This is remarkably close to the observed LB score of **~306,800**, and certainly much closer than the simple `KFold` CV score was.

The small remaining difference of about 1,000 could be attributed to several factors. It's possible the actual proportion of "future" data in the Public LB is slightly higher than 1.42%, or it could simply reflect a minor degree of model overfitting.

Regardless, this calculation presents a plausible theory. It suggests the CV-LB gap isn't a random offset, but can be quantitatively explained by the performance drop on the small, but extremely challenging, future portion of the test set.

Feature Fixing

- I impute `sale_nbr`, which is the number of resales, with 0. Since the unique values of `sale_nbr` didn't include 0, I thought it would be reasonable to assume that nulls meant no resales.

- I filtered for houses where `sqft` (living area) < `sqft_1` (first floor area), and replaced `sqft` with `sqft_1 + sqft_fbsmt`.

Adding Features

I did a lot of these, the implementation details will be in my notebook (that I hopefully eventually publish). A summary (not exhaustive):

- Time based: 

- `sale_epoch`, year, month, season, quarter, etc. Having either `sale_epoch` or `sale_year` is extermely important; simply adding `sale_year` to the [starter demo](https://www.kaggle.com/code/reidjohnson/pi-ii-demo-qrf) instantly boosts the score from 90k to something like 40k.

- How many years apart to sale the house was from built/renovated/joined as `built/reno/join_ydiff`

- Tax values:

- Sum up `imp_val` (improvement tax) and `land_val` (land tax) as `imp_p_land`

- Location based: 

- I use KNN fitted on `latitude` and `longitude` and regress for `sqft, sqft_lot, grade, condition, imp_p_land, imp_val, land_val`. I don't regress for `sale_price` as I found this easily overfits.

- I find `latitude, longitude` of types of locations (tech centers, tourist attractions, top schools), and create features that's smallest distance from the house to a location of that type.

- I average up the views as `avg_views`, and an alternate version where I only include the non-zero ones in the calculation as `...nz...`. I additionally consider `golf, wfnt, greenbelt` along with the views as `lux` (luxury). I average the water-related ones as `water`.

- House properties:

- `sqft_2_above`, `sqft_per_bed`, `sqft_per_bath`, `bed_per_bath`, etc. to loosely capture the interior design.

- `floor_area_ratio = sqft / sqft_lot` and `build_cover_ratio = sqft_1 / sqft_lot`, 2 measures of buildings when I was looking for info on google.

- Cross Category:

- concatenate `(grade, condition)` and also `(city, area)` to model categorical feature interactions

- Encoding: 

- To prevent too many dimensions, I represent `sale_warning` as bitmasks, i.e. `[has_code_26, has_code_15, has_code_40, ...]`, and treat that as the binary representation of an integer, resulting in 1 column fully representing the different combinations of `sale_warning`s.

- I extract the string part and number part of `zoning` into `zoning_str` and `zoning_n`, then reference [King County zoning](https://kingcounty.gov/en/dept/kcit/data-information-services/gis-center/property-research/zoning), [Seattle zoning](https://www.seattle.gov/sdci/codes/codes-we-enforce-(a-z)/zoning), [Bellevue zoning](https://bellevue.municipal.codes/LUC/20.10), etc. to create `zoning_type` that's Residential, Commercial, etc

- `subdivision` was `TargetEncoder`ed, the rest `OrdinalEncoder`ed.

Modeling

Two `CatBoostRegressor()`s were employed to predict the 5th and 95th quantile. A validation set is split from the training set to perform conformalization. 

Empirically I found that rather than using 1 correction term `q`, it was more accurate to split into `q_high` and `q_low`, i.e. 

`

nonconf_low = calib_preds[] - y_calib
nonconf_high = y_calib - calib_preds[]
q_low = np.quantile(nonconf_low, ( - alpha / ))
q_high = np.quantile(nonconf_high, ( - alpha / ))

 p  [train_preds, calib_preds, test_preds]:
    p[] -= q_low
    p[] += q_high
`

Potential Future Improvements

- **Better feature fixing:** I discovered some other abnormalties like houses having 0 beds or 33 or 54 beds too late without time on my hands to fix those.

- **Better feature engineering:**

- For example, I only included 4 tech centers (amazon hq, microsoft hq, meta hq, google 6 st camp), which is by no means extensive.

- I used haversine distances in my calculations, but a more accurate measure should be travel distance, i.e. the actual time it'd take for someone to say drive from A to B. To achieve this, maybe something like OpenStreetMap could've been used.

- I didn't investigate some other curiosities, like `wfnt` (waterfront quality) vs. `sale_price` forming a reverse-U-shape, meaning that the highest waterfront quality properties sold for less. Discovering the reasons behind this could lead to better engineering.

- **Correct usage of models:** 

- To speed up training, I `OrdinalEncoder`ed every remaining categorical variable and treated them like numbers, rather than leaving them as categoricals and specifying `cat_features` in my `CatBoostRegressor`. This empirically lead to easier overfitting but reduced my training time by half.

- Again to speed up training, I used euclidean distance when calculating neighbors using KNNs. Using a more accurate distance could help.

- **Better model:** Compared to the sophisticated ensembles and model tuning other people did, my modeling is pretty sloppy - it's a simple CQR, and I basically winged the hyperparameters manually without doing any hyperopt afterwards. Switching to a more powerful model could probably improve scores.

External Data Experiment (CV/LB: ~220000)

As revealed in [this discussion](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/prediction-interval-competition-ii-house-price/discussion/591388), additional external data exists for this competition which appears to contain some target-related ("leaky") information. I created a notebook purely out of my own curiosity to explore the impact of this data on model performance, and I'm sharing my approach and results here.

Strategy: KNN Feature Engineering

The core intent is to leverage the external data by finding properties within it that are most similar to those in our training and test sets. By doing so, we can create powerful new features based on their sales prices.

Implementation Details

The process is broken down into the following steps:

- **Data Aggregation and Cleaning**: First, all external data files are loaded and concatenated into a single reference dataset. Duplicate records are then removed to ensure the quality of our lookup data.

- **Feature Selection and Scaling**: A set of key numerical features that describe the physical and geographical characteristics of a property (e.g., `latitude`, `longitude`, `sqft`, `grade`) is selected to define "similarity". These features are standardized using `StandardScaler` to ensure that no single feature with a large scale disproportionately influences the distance calculation.

- **Accelerated Neighbor Search**: We use `cuml.neighbors.NearestNeighbors` to take advantage of GPU acceleration, which significantly speeds up the process of finding the nearest neighbors. For each property in the main `train` and `test` sets, this step identifies the `k` most similar properties from the aggregated external dataset (for k=1, 3, 5).

- **Feature Creation**: Once the nearest neighbors are identified, we generate several new features based on the neighbors' attributes:

- `knn_price_mean_k*`: The average `sale_price` of the `k` neighbors.

- `knn_price_std_k*`: The standard deviation of the neighbors' `sale_price`, acting as a measure of price variance.

- `knn_dist_mean_k*` / `knn_dist_max_k*`: The mean and maximum distances to the `k` neighbors, quantifying the quality of the match.

Final Stacking Model

Finally, these newly created KNN features are appended to the out-of-fold (OOF) predictions from a diverse set of base models. This combined feature set is then used to train the final Level 2 PyTorch neural network, which predicts the upper and lower bounds of the price interval.

Results

The final model achieved:

- **CV Score: ~220,000**

- **Public(Private) LB Score: ~220,000**

This result confirms that the features derived from the external data provide a significant performance boost.
