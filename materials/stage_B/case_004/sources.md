## Problem

When a wildfire ignites, emergency managers and responders must decide which communities to warn, when to warn them, and where to position scarce resources. Decisions must be made before certainty is available. The response requires both prioritization (which fires are most urgent soon) and calibrated risk estimates (how likely a fire is to threaten evacuation zones within actionable time windows). 

This competition turns that operational need into a survival analysis challenge. You will generate calibrated probability forecasts across multiple time horizons to support real-world decisions.

## Real-World Context
Many wildfire forecasting approaches reduce the task to a single question. Will this fire become dangerous. Emergency response needs more. Decisions are time-bound and comparative. How soon is a threat likely. How confident should we be. Which incident should move to the front of the queue when crews, aircraft, and messaging capacity are limited.

This competition addresses that gap by framing the task as survival analysis using real early-incident signals. The dataset captures wildfire perimeter dynamics and their spatial relationship to evacuation zones. Features are computed strictly from the first five hours after the initial perimeter observation, t0. The target is the time from t0 plus 5 hours until the fire comes within 5 km of any evacuation zone centroid.

## Survival Analysis Framing and Censoring
This is right-censored survival analysis:
- If a fire hits within the 72-hour window, event = 1 and time_to_hit_hours is the observed time from t0+5h.
- If a fire does not hit within 72 hours, event = 0 and time_to_hit_hours is the last observed time in the window (<= 72).

Participants do not predict a single time. They submit survival probabilities that a hit occurs by 12h, 24h, 48h, and 72h. The strongest solutions will deliver two outcomes at once. They will rank fires correctly by urgency for triage and produce calibrated probabilities that can support threshold-based decisions.

Primary Metric:

**Hybrid Score = 0.3 x C-index + 0.7 x (1 - Weighted Brier Score)**

## C-index (30%)
- Measures how well you rank fires by urgency
- Higher is better (0.5 to 1.0)

## Weighted Brier Score (70%)
- Measures calibration at 24h, 48h, 72h
- Uses censor-aware evaluation:
  - Hits: 1 if hit by horizon H, else 0
  - Censored after H: 0
  - Censored before H: excluded
- Final weighted average:
  0.3 x Brier@24h + 0.4 x Brier@48h + 0.3 x Brier@72h

### Why 48h Is Weighted Highest
- 24-48 hours is the strongest operational value zone
- 48h is emphasized because it balances actionable lead time with decision urgency.
- 72h is included for extended planning, but it can be less operationally immediate than earlier horizons.

Hello,

I'm one of the hosts for the competition and wanted to provide clarification and respond to your questions:
    •    Meaning of censored data (event = 0):
    •    This is a right-censored survival setup with a 72-hour observation window.
    •    event = 1 means the fire reached the evacuation zone within the 72-hour window.
    •    event = 0 means the fire did not reach the zone during the observation window. In that case, time_to_hit_hours is the last time we observed the fire within the window (so it’s not the true time of a hit that might occur later).

**Why there appear to be “two descriptions” of censoring:**
    •    These are describing two different things: the data semantics vs. the evaluation rule.
    •    The dataset description explains what time_to_hit_hours and event mean in the solution/labels.
    •    The metric description explains how we convert those values into horizon-specific labels for evaluation.
    •    So there isn’t a contradiction: one is “what the fields mean,” and the other is “how we score predictions at a given horizon.”

**How horizon-based Brier scoring handles censoring:**
    •    For each horizon H, we score whether the fire hit by H.
    •    If the fire hit by horizon H, it’s treated as a positive (label 1).
    •    If the fire is censored but its last observed time is after horizon H, it’s treated as a negative for that horizon (label 0), because we know it did not hit by H.
    •    If the fire is censored before horizon H, it’s excluded from the Brier calculation at that horizon, because we don’t have enough information to say whether it would have hit by H.

**How the C-index is computed:**
    •    The C-index is computed once using time_to_hit_hours, event, and the 12-hour risk score from the submission (it is not averaged across horizons, so case2).

    •   This is a right-censored survival dataset with a 72-hour observation window.
    •   event = 1 means a hit was observed within that window.
    •   event = 0 means a hit was not observed within the window. Importantly, time_to_hit_hours is the last time we observed the situation within the window, not the time of a hit. After that point, we simply don’t have an observed outcome in this dataset row.

    •   Even when we don’t observe a hit, the last observed time tells you how long the fire-zone situation was tracked without a hit being observed.
    •   A censored row with a longer last-observed time provides stronger evidence that “no hit occurred up to at least that time” than a censored row with a shorter last-observed time.     •   What we can say from event = 0 is: “no hit was observed within the 72-hour window.”

4th Place Solution – WiDS Global Datathon 2026 

**Overview**

This competition focused on predicting the probability that a wildfire would threaten an evacuation zone within 12h, 24h, 48h, and 72h using only the first five hours of wildfire behavior data.

I approached the problem as a right-censored survival analysis task since the dataset contained both observed event times and censored wildfire events.

The final solution combined:

- survival models,

- physics-inspired feature engineering,

- distance-stratified ensembling,

- and a simple 72h post-processing rule.

The solution achieved **4th place globally**.

**Key Insight**

The most important observation was that wildfire threat progression was primarily driven by:

- distance to evacuation zones,

- closing speed,

- radial growth,

- directional alignment,

- and movement toward or away from evacuation zones.

Instead of broad feature expansion, I focused on modeling the physical relationship between wildfire spread and evacuation-zone proximity.

**Feature Engineering**

I created a compact set of physics-inspired features designed to capture wildfire dynamics, including:

- estimated time-to-reach evacuation zone,

- radial growth relative to distance,

- area growth relative to distance,

- directional risk,

- distance change ratio,

- log-distance,

- log positive closing speed,

- and spread pressure.

These features helped the survival models better capture urgency and risk progression while avoiding overfitting on the small dataset.

**Models**

The final ensemble used three survival models:

**Gradient Boosting Survival Analysis (GBSA)**

The strongest standalone model, particularly effective at capturing nonlinear wildfire behavior.

**Random Survival Forest (RSF)**

Added model diversity and improved robustness across different wildfire regimes.

**CoxNet**

Provided a more linear survival perspective and performed especially well for far-distance fires.

**Cross-Validation**

I used 5-fold cross-validation to:

- generate out-of-fold predictions,

- produce stable test predictions,

- and support ensemble optimization.

Test predictions were averaged across folds.

**Distance-Stratified Ensemble**

One of the most impactful improvements came from stratifying fires into:

- near,

- medium,

- and far-distance groups

using tertiles of the minimum evacuation-zone distance feature.

Instead of using one global ensemble weight, I optimized separate model weights for each distance bin using Optuna.

This worked well because different models performed better in different distance regimes:

- near and medium-distance fires relied heavily on GBSA,

- while far-distance fires benefited more from CoxNet.

**72h Post-Processing**

The final solution applied a simple rule:

- Set all 72h probabilities to 1.0.

Although simple, this consistently improved leaderboard performance because the 72h horizon behaved differently from shorter horizons and was strongly skewed toward positive outcomes among observed events.

The model predictions were still fully used for the 12h, 24h, and 48h horizons.

**Monotonicity Enforcement**

Final predictions were constrained to ensure:

- probability(12h) ≤ probability(24h) ≤ probability(48h) ≤ probability(72h)

This ensured logical consistency across time horizons.

**What Did Not Work**

Several approaches were tested but did not improve the final score, including:

- isotonic calibration,

- broader feature expansion,

- cyclical time features,

- LightGBM horizon models,

- horizon-wise ensemble tuning,

- and threshold-based 72h post-processing.

The simpler distance-stratified survival ensemble remained the strongest solution.

**Final Pipeline**

- Load train and test data

- Create physics-inspired features

- Create distance bins using training tertiles

- Train GBSA, RSF, and CoxNet using 5-fold CV

- Generate OOF and test predictions

- Optimize ensemble weights per distance bin using Optuna

- Blend predictions using bin-specific weights

- Apply 72h override

- Enforce monotonicity
-Generate final submission

Overview

This competition focused on predicting when a wildfire may threaten an evacuation zone. For each fire, we estimated the probability of a threat within 12, 24, 48, and 72 hours using only the first five hours of fire perimeter information.

This was a right-censored survival prediction problem. Some fires reached an evacuation zone, while others did not during the observation window. For censored fires, we only know that a hit was not observed during the available time. Because of this, the model had to handle event timing and censoring while still producing calibrated probabilities for fixed horizons.

Our approach was to build a horizon-specific ensemble. We engineered features for distance, movement, fire growth, alignment, and estimated time to reach an evacuation zone. Then we trained multiple model families, combined their outputs, and applied post-processing so the final probabilities stayed valid and followed the required cumulative order over time.

Main Idea

The main idea was to avoid treating the task as one single classification problem. Since the competition required probabilities at four horizons, we built the solution around those horizons directly. A fire that may threaten an evacuation zone within 12 hours can behave differently from one that becomes risky closer to 48 or 72 hours.

We also handled censoring carefully. For each horizon, fires censored before that horizon were not treated as clear negatives because their true outcome was unknown. We built horizon-specific labels only from examples where the outcome was known and used censor-aware weighting to reduce bias.

The final solution combined two views of the problem. Horizon-specific models helped produce calibrated probabilities for fixed time windows, while survival-risk models helped capture the overall urgency ranking of fires. This matched the competition metric, which rewarded both ranking and probability calibration.

Feature Engineering

Feature engineering was important because the original columns did not fully capture the relationship between the fire, its movement, and the nearest evacuation zone. We created features that described closeness, movement, growth, direction, and estimated arrival time.

The engineered features included distance transformations such as distance in kilometers, log distance, square-root distance, inverse distance, and distance-zone indicators. We also added distance trend features such as normalized distance change, normalized distance slope, normalized distance variation, distance acceleration, and a distance-trend reliability flag.

We created movement and ETA-based features using closing speed, radial growth rate, effective closing speed, and along-track speed. From these, we estimated time-to-arrival values and horizon-specific ETA indicators for 12, 24, 48, and 72 hours. We also added fire size and growth features such as estimated fire radius, radius-to-distance ratio, area-to-distance ratio, growth-to-distance ratio, and radial-growth-to-distance ratio.

Finally, we added directional and temporal features, including alignment with the evacuation zone, alignment and closing-speed interactions, threat pressure, cross-track movement, bearing strength, movement flags, perimeter density, short-window indicators, low temporal resolution indicators, and cyclical encodings for hour, day of week, and month.

Modeling Approach

Our final approach was a seed-bagged, horizon-specific ensemble with survival-risk blending and monotonic post-processing. Since the required outputs were cumulative probabilities at 12, 24, 48, and 72 hours, we trained the model around those time horizons instead of using one general prediction.

We used LightGBM, XGBoost, and CatBoost as horizon-specific tree-based models. For 12 and 24 hours, these models predicted whether a fire would threaten an evacuation zone by that time. For 48 hours, we used a tail-modeling approach to estimate the additional probability of a threat between 24 and 48 hours. The 72-hour probability was extended from the 48-hour probability using the late-event rate from the training data.

Because the dataset included censored examples, we used censor-aware training and inverse probability of censoring weighting. This helped avoid treating unknown outcomes as negatives and made the usable examples better represent the full training distribution.

We also trained XGBoost Cox and XGBoost AFT models to capture the time-to-event structure. Their outputs were converted into horizon probabilities using isotonic calibration and combined into one survival-risk component. The final prediction blended four components: LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost, and the Cox/AFT survival-risk component. Multiple random seeds were averaged for the tree-based models to improve stability.

After blending, we applied post-processing. The 12-hour probability received an extra calibration adjustment because the earliest horizon was the most sensitive. We then applied a final consistency check so the cumulative probabilities followed the expected time ordering across the four horizons.

Validation and Metric

We used a local version of the competition metric to compare modeling ideas. The metric combined C-index and weighted Brier score. C-index measured how well the model ranked fires by urgency, while the weighted Brier score measured probability accuracy at the 24-hour, 48-hour, and 72-hour horizons.

For the Brier score, censoring was handled by horizon. Fires that hit before the horizon were labeled as 1, fires still observed past the horizon without hitting were labeled as 0, and fires censored before the horizon were excluded because their true outcome was unknown.

Since the final score placed more weight on probability calibration than ranking, we focused on stable and well-calibrated probabilities. This is why horizon-specific models, censor-aware labels, calibration, and post-processing were important.
